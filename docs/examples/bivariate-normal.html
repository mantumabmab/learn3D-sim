The bivariate Normal surface

A joint density over two variables is a surface, and almost everything about the bivariate Normal is a statement about that surface's geometry: its level sets are ellipses determined by the eigenstructure of Σ, its shadows are the marginals, and its vertical cross-sections are — up to a constant — the conditionals. The figure below is a real renderer, not an illustration: rotate it, change Σ, slice it, and read the numbers off.

Covered here
  • the density in matrix form, and why the exponent must be a quadratic form;
  • how the eigen-decomposition of Σ fixes the orientation and size of the probability ellipses;
  • marginals as projections and conditionals as normalised slices — and why those are different operations;
  • the regression line falling out of the conditional mean, with 1 − ρ² as the variance reduction;
  • what a 3D surface plot genuinely shows and what it systematically misleads about.

1. The density

For X = (X1, X2)T with mean μ and covariance Σ, the density is

f(x) = (2π)−1 |Σ|−1/2 exp( −½ (x − μ)T Σ−1 (x − μ) )

Written out in the scalar parameterisation (μ1, μ2, σ1, σ2, ρ):

f(x1,x2) = 1/(2πσ1σ2√(1−ρ²)) · exp( −Q / (2(1−ρ²)) )
Q = z1² + z2² − 2ρz1z2,   zi = (xi − μi)/σi

The exponent is a negative-definite quadratic form, and that single fact generates the entire geometry. Because f depends on x only through the scalar (x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ), every level set {x : f(x) = c} is a level set of that quadratic form — an ellipse. Contours of a bivariate Normal are never anything else.

2. The geometry of Σ

This is the part worth internalising: Σ is not a bag of three numbers, it is an ellipse.

The quantity in the exponent is the squared Mahalanobis distance, D² = (x−μ)TΣ−1(x−μ). Under bivariate normality it is distributed as χ² with 2 degrees of freedom, which is what makes probability ellipses computable: the region D² ≤ c has probability P(χ²2 ≤ c).

In two dimensions that has a clean closed form, because the χ²2 distribution is exponential with mean 2:

P(D² ≤ c) = 1 − e−c/2  ⇒   95% ellipse at c = 5.9915,  √c = 2.4477

Diagonalise: Σ = UΛUT. The eigenvectors give the ellipse's axis directions and the eigenvalues their squared lengths, so the semi-axes of the probability-p ellipse are √(cλi). For the 2×2 case,

λ± = ½[ σ1² + σ2² ± √((σ1²−σ2²)² + 4ρ²σ1²σ2²) ],   θ = ½ atan2(2ρσ1σ2, σ1²−σ2²)

Two limits are worth holding. At ρ = 0 the axes are parallel to the coordinate axes with half-lengths σ1 and σ2. As ρ → ±1 one eigenvalue goes to zero, the ellipse collapses to a line segment, |Σ| → 0, and the density degenerates — there is no two-dimensional density left, because the mass concentrates on a line.

The slice is not the conditional.

Cutting the surface at x1 = c gives the function x2 ↦ f(c, x2). That is proportional to the conditional density, but it does not integrate to 1 — it integrates to the marginal value f1(c). To get the conditional you divide:

f(x2|c) = f(c, x2) / f1(c)

Set ρ to 0 and orbit to the top view: the ellipse becomes axis-aligned. Push ρ toward 0.95 and watch the surface collapse onto a ridge while |Σ| heads to zero. Predict before you do it: as you slide the cut position c away from μ1, does the conditional curve get wider, narrower, or stay the same width?

4. Marginals are projections

Integrating out x2 collapses the surface onto the x1 axis:

f1(x1) = ∫ f(x1,x2) dx2 = N(μ1, σ1²)

The correlation has vanished. That is the single most important thing about the marginals, and the figure makes it visible: slide ρ from 0 to 0.95 and the marginal curves drawn on the back walls do not move at all, while the surface between them changes completely. Correlation is a statement about the joint distribution that no marginal can see.

This is why the marginal is not the slice through the peak. The marginal at x1 is the total mass in that column — the area of the cut — not the height of the surface along the ridge.

5. Conditionals, and why this is regression

Normalising the slice gives a Normal distribution again:

X2 | X1 = x  ~  N( μ2 + ρ(σ2/σ1)(x − μ1),  σ2²(1 − ρ²) )

Read the two pieces separately.

The mean is linear in x. It traces the line through (μ1, μ2) with slope ρσ2/σ1 — exactly the population least-squares regression coefficient. Simple linear regression is not an approximation imposed on bivariate Normal data; it is the conditional mean, exactly.

The variance does not depend on x. This is homoscedasticity, and it is a theorem here rather than an assumption: every slice has the same width σ2√(1−ρ²), however far out you cut. The factor 1 − ρ² is 1 − R² — the proportion of variance left unexplained.

The regression-to-the-mean reading.

Standardise both variables (σ1 = σ2 = 1) and the conditional mean becomes ρx. Since |ρ| < 1, the expected value of X2 is always closer to the mean, in standard units, than the observed x was. Galton's regression to mediocrity is this line, and it is a property of the bivariate Normal's geometry rather than any causal mechanism.

6. What a 3D surface plot hides

Since the whole point of this page is a 3D rendering, it is worth being blunt about its limitations.

ProblemWhat goes wrongWhat to do
OcclusionThe near side of the hill hides the far side. A second mode, or a lump in the tail, can be entirely invisible from the default camera angle.Orbit before concluding anything. Better: use a contour plot, where nothing is hidden.
Slice vs conditionalA cut through the surface looks like a conditional distribution but has the wrong area, by a factor of f1(c) that varies with where you cut.Renormalise before comparing cuts at different c, as the inset does.
Height is not probabilityDensity is not probability; only its integral is. The peak height 1/(2πσ1σ2√(1−ρ²)) grows without bound as ρ → ±1 — the surface gets taller while the distribution gets less informative about nothing in particular.Read volumes, not heights. The ellipse gives you volume directly.
Perspective distorts comparisonEqual heights at different depths project to different screen sizes, so visual comparison of two bumps is unreliable.Use the top view for anything quantitative.

The honest summary: a 3D surface is excellent for building intuition about shape and poor for reading values. Contour plots and marginal/conditional panels do the quantitative work. That is why the figure above ships a top view button and a 2D inset.

7. Beyond the basics

  • Sampling by Cholesky. Factor Σ = LLT; then X = μ + LZ with Z ~ N(0, I). In 2D, L = [[σ1, 0], [ρσ2, σ2√(1−ρ²)]] — and the √(1−ρ²) appearing again is not a coincidence; it is the conditional standard deviation. The figure uses exactly this, with a fixed set of standard normal draws, which is why the cloud deforms continuously as you move the sliders rather than resampling.
  • Zero correlation and independence. For a jointly Normal pair, ρ = 0 implies independence, because Σ becomes diagonal and the density factorises. This fails in general: marginally Normal variables with zero correlation need not be independent, and need not be jointly Normal. The usable characterisation is that (X1, X2) is bivariate Normal if and only if aX1 + bX2 + c is univariate Normal for every choice of a, b, c.
  • Dimension. Everything above generalises with D² ~ χ²k and ellipsoids instead of ellipses, but the picture stops helping. In high dimensions a Gaussian's mass sits in a thin shell at radius ≈ σ√k, not near the mode — the mode is the least typical place to be. Two-dimensional intuition actively misleads here.
  • When the Normal shape is wrong but the dependence is right. Copulas separate the marginals from the dependence structure; the Gaussian copula keeps this elliptical dependence while allowing arbitrary marginals. Its failure to capture joint tail dependence is a well-documented problem in risk modelling.

Self-test

You increase ρ from 0.3 to 0.9, holding everything else fixed. What happens to the marginal of X₁, the conditional variance of X₂ given X₁, and the peak height?

The marginal of X₁ is unchanged — still N(μ₁, σ₁²); ρ does not appear in it. The conditional variance falls from σ₂²(1−0.09) = 0.91σ₂² to σ₂²(1−0.81) = 0.19σ₂², a large tightening. The peak height 1/(2πσ₁σ₂√(1−ρ²)) rises by a factor √(0.91/0.19) ≈ 2.19, because the same unit of mass is being squeezed onto a narrower ridge.

Why is the conditional variance independent of where you slice, and would you expect that to survive if the joint distribution were not Normal?

It follows from the exponent being exactly quadratic: completing the square in x₂ leaves a quadratic whose coefficient does not involve x₁, so the width of every slice is the same. It is a special property, not a general one. For most non-Normal joint distributions the conditional spread varies with the conditioning value — that is heteroscedasticity, and it is the normal state of affairs outside the Gaussian world. Homoscedasticity in regression is routinely treated as an assumption to be checked precisely because only the Gaussian case guarantees it.

A colleague shows a 3D density surface and says the two variables are "clearly independent because the hill looks symmetric". What is wrong?

Visual symmetry of the rendered hill is not the right criterion, and the camera angle can create or destroy the appearance of tilt. Independence for a bivariate Normal means ρ = 0, i.e. the contour ellipses are axis-aligned — which is a statement best checked from the top view, not an oblique one. And if the distribution is not known to be jointly Normal, even a genuinely axis-aligned appearance would not establish independence, only zero correlation.

Why do the semi-axes scale as √(cλᵢ) rather than λᵢ?

Because the eigenvalues of Σ are variances, which live in squared units. The ellipse (x−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x−μ) = c written in the eigenbasis is Σᵢ yᵢ²/λᵢ = c, so the semi-axis along direction i is √(cλᵢ). At c = 1 and ρ = 0 that recovers the familiar σ₁ and σ₂.
