How a mass spectrometer works

Postgraduate notes · the mechanism end to end · about 25 minutes, with a simulation you can drive

A mass spectrometer does three things in sequence, and almost every instrument ever built is a different answer to the same three questions: how do you turn a neutral molecule into an ion, how do you sort the ions by mass-to-charge ratio, and how do you count what arrives. These notes follow one complete chain — an electron-ionisation source, a mass analyser, a detector — and then open out the middle stage, because that is where the instruments differ.

By the end you should be able to: write down why flight time, bending radius and oscillation frequency all scale as √(m/z) and say what that implies; predict which ions a quadrupole transmits from the Mathieu parameters; explain why a reflectron improves resolution without improving the mass range; and read an isotope pattern as a statement about elemental composition.

1. What the instrument actually measures

It measures mass-to-charge ratio, not mass. Every equation below contains m/z as a single quantity, because an ion's response to an electric or magnetic field depends on its inertia and its charge together and on nothing else. A doubly charged ion of 400 Da is indistinguishable, in the analyser, from a singly charged ion of 200 Da; the two are told apart only by the spacing of their isotope peaks, which is 1/z Da.

The second thing to hold onto is that the whole chain runs in high vacuum, typically 10−5 to 10−6 torr in the source and lower in the analyser.[1] This is not fastidiousness: the mean free path has to exceed the flight path, or ions collide, scatter and lose the velocity or phase that encodes their mass. Vacuum is a precondition for the measurement, not a convenience.

2. Making the ions: electron ionisation

A heated filament emits electrons, which are accelerated through 70 V across the ionisation volume and cross the stream of neutral analyte. An interaction ejects an electron from the molecule:

M + e− → M+• + 2e− The product is a radical cation — odd-electron, and usually in a highly excited vibrational state.

Why 70 eV, universally? Two reasons, and it is worth separating them. First, 70 eV sits on the broad plateau of the ionisation cross-section, so small drifts in filament potential barely change the spectrum; this is what makes 70 eV spectra reproducible between laboratories and between instrument types, and it is the reason the great reference libraries are all recorded at that value.[2][3] Second, 70 eV is enormous compared with what the molecule needs. Ionising a typical organic compound costs about 10 eV, while a typical single bond is worth roughly 3 eV, so the excess energy deposited — around 15 eV in a typical event — drives extensive fragmentation.[2]

That fragmentation is the point and the price. It is the point because the fragment pattern is a structural fingerprint, reproducible enough to search against a library. It is the price because for many compounds the molecular ion does not survive at all, so the one number you most wanted — the molecular mass — is missing.[2] Soft ionisation methods (chemical ionisation, electrospray, MALDI) exist precisely to buy the molecular ion back, at the cost of the fingerprint.

Worth labelling The older name "electron impact" survives in common use but is mechanistically misleading: a 70 eV electron passes a molecule in roughly 10−16 s and transfers energy through the perturbation of its field rather than through a billiard-ball collision. IUPAC recommends "electron ionisation" for this reason.[4]

3. The one equation the rest depends on

Ions leave the source and fall through an accelerating potential V. All the electrical work becomes kinetic energy:

zeV = ½mv² → v = √(2zeV / m) Every ion acquires the same energy, so heavier ions acquire a lower speed — and speed is what every analyser downstream converts into a measurement.

From that single relation the three classical analysers follow immediately. Let an ion drift a distance L: the flight time is t = L√(m / 2zeV), proportional to √(m/z). Bend it in a magnetic field B: the Lorentz force sets r = mv/zeB = √(2Vm/z)/B, again proportional to √(m/z). The Orbitrap, which does not accelerate ions to a common energy at all, nonetheless reports a frequency going as 1/√(m/z). The square root is not a coincidence between three designs; it is the same relation read three ways.

Magnetic sector — dispersion in space

An ion entering a uniform field perpendicular to its velocity follows a circular arc of radius r = √(2Vm/z)/B. The exit slit sits at one fixed radius, so only one m/z can pass at a time; the spectrum is swept by ramping B (or V). A sector is a genuine momentum-and-energy filter, which is why it was for decades the reference for accurate mass. It is also large, heavy, and intrinsically a scanning instrument: while one m/z reaches the detector, every other ion is being thrown away.

Quadrupole — dispersion in stability

Four parallel rods carry ±(U + V cos Ωt). In the resulting field, transverse ion motion obeys the Mathieu equation, and the behaviour depends only on two dimensionless parameters:

a = 8zeU / (m r₀² Ω²) q = 4zeV / (m r₀² Ω²) Both scale as 1/m, so for fixed U, V, r₀ and Ω every ion sits at its own point on a line through the origin of slope a/q = 2U/V.

Bounded trajectories exist only inside a stability region whose apex sits at q = 0.70600, a = 0.23699; the region meets the q axis at q = 0.90805.[6][7] Ions outside it gain amplitude exponentially and strike a rod. Because the operating point is a straight line through the origin, the whole spectrum is scanned by ramping U and V together at constant ratio, and that ratio alone sets the resolution.

This is the part worth sitting with. A line drawn exactly through the apex is tangent to the region and transmits nothing. Lower its slope slightly and it cuts a short chord: a narrow window of q, hence a narrow window of m/z. Numerically, operating at a fraction f of the apex slope gives roughly

R ≈ 0.75 / (1 − f) Verified by locating the transmitted q window from the Mathieu characteristic values: 1−f = 0.01 → R ≈ 75; 0.001 → 737; 0.0001 → 6200.

So unit resolution at m/z 300 demands that the DC-to-RF ratio be held within about 0.25% of the apex value, and higher resolution is bought directly in transmitted ion current. That trade — resolution against sensitivity, set by one voltage ratio — is the defining characteristic of a quadrupole, and the reason its resolving power sits in the hundreds rather than the thousands.[8]

Time of flight — dispersion in time

Pulse the ions, let them drift a field-free distance, and time their arrival: t = L√(m / 2zeV). Every ion is measured in every pulse, so unlike a scanning analyser nothing is discarded, and the mass range is unbounded in principle. The limit is timing. Since m ∝ t², the resolving power is

R = m / Δm = t / 2Δt A 1 m tube at 20 kV puts m/z 500 at the detector in 11.4 µs; a 1 ns peak width then gives R ≈ 5700.

The dominant contribution to Δt is not electronics but the spread of initial kinetic energies in the source: ions of the same mass leave with slightly different speeds and arrive smeared. Linear instruments are held to roughly a few hundred in resolving power by this alone.[9][10]

The reflectron fixes it with a trick worth admiring. An ion entering a retarding field decelerates, stops, and returns; the time spent inside is 2mv/zeE, proportional to the entry speed. A hotter ion overtakes its cohort in the drift tube and then penetrates deeper into the mirror and spends correspondingly longer there. To first order the two effects cancel and ions of equal m/z arrive together regardless of their initial energy.[11] The mirror also folds the flight path, which lengthens t without lengthening the instrument.

The simulation lets you see the size of that effect: at ±15 eV of initial energy spread the linear instrument smears m/z 77 over about 16 ns, and switching the mirror on collapses that to under 10 ps. Do not read the resulting six-figure resolving power as a specification. It is the contribution of this one term, and once it falls below the others — the turn-around time of ions initially moving the wrong way, the spatial spread in the source, detector rise time, field imperfections — those become the limit. That is why real single-stage reflectron instruments land near 104 rather than 106.

Try this Switch to time of flight, set the energy spread to ±40 eV and turn the reflectron off. Read the peak width in the readout. Now switch the reflectron on without touching anything else. Predict first: does the arrival time get shorter or longer, and what happens to the width?

Orbitrap — dispersion in frequency

Ions orbit a spindle-shaped central electrode inside a barrel, in a field engineered so that the axial potential is exactly harmonic. The ion's axial oscillation therefore has frequency

ω = √(k / (m/z)) k is the field curvature, fixed by the electrode geometry.

The crucial property is that this axial frequency is independent of the ion's energy and of its spatial spread.[12][13] That is what removes the limit every other analyser runs into: a sector must be fed a monoenergetic beam, a TOF must correct for energy spread, but the Orbitrap simply does not care. The oscillating ions induce an image current on the two halves of the outer electrode; the transient is Fourier-transformed, and frequency maps to m/z.

Because frequency is measured rather than position or arrival, resolution is bought with time: a longer transient resolves closer frequencies. At a fixed acquisition time the resolving power falls as 1/√(m/z) — more slowly than FT-ICR, where it falls as 1/(m/z), which is why an Orbitrap at 60,000 and a 7 T FT-ICR at 100,000 (both quoted at m/z 400) cross over near m/z 1100.[14]

Try this On the Orbitrap, set the transient to 16 µs and look at the peak near 112. Then drag it to 1200 µs. Predict first: the two frequencies differ by 6.1 kHz, so how long must you listen before you can tell them apart? Check your answer against the resolving power in the readout.

Note what you are watching. Nothing about the ions changed — the same ions are still orbiting the same way. All that changed is how long you listened to them. That is what it means to buy resolution with time.

5. Counting what arrives

Two detection principles, matched to the two kinds of analyser.

Destructive counting. Beam instruments use an electron multiplier: the ion strikes a conversion dynode, liberated secondary electrons are accelerated into the next surface, and the cascade delivers a gain of around 106–108, enough to register a single ion as a usable pulse. The ion is consumed in the measurement.

Non-destructive sensing. Trapping instruments detect the image current that moving ions induce on nearby electrodes. Nothing is absorbed, so the same ion population can be observed for the whole transient — which is exactly why resolution scales with acquisition time. The signal is a sum of sinusoids and means nothing until it is Fourier-transformed; the "spectrum" is a derived quantity, not a direct record of arrivals. One consequence worth knowing: a transient is a finite record, so its transform carries the side lobes of whatever window was applied. Real instruments apodise before transforming (the simulation uses a Hann window) which suppresses the lobes at the cost of roughly doubling the peak width — so quoted resolving power depends on the window as well as on the acquisition time. In the simulation, switching to the Orbitrap switches the spectrum panel from a histogram of counts to the magnitude spectrum of a transient, which is a real difference in what is being plotted.

6. Reading the spectrum

Isotope patterns are compositional evidence

Peaks above the monoisotopic mass are not noise; they are a binomial distribution over the heavy isotopes present, and they constrain composition before you have done any interpretation at all.

  • Carbon. 13C is 1.07% abundant,[5] so for n carbons the M+1 peak is about 1.07n per cent of M. Counting carbons from the M+1 height is a standard first move, accurate to about one carbon for n below roughly 20.
  • Chlorine. 35Cl:37Cl is 75.78:24.22, giving M:M+2 of 3.13:1 for one chlorine.[5] Two chlorines give 1 : 0.639 : 0.102 — a signature you can recognise across a room.
  • Bromine. 50.69:49.31, so M and M+2 are near enough equal.[5]
  • Sulfur. 34S at 4.29% raises M+2 by about 4.4% per sulfur, which is a much weaker signal and is easily lost under an M+2 from two 13C.[5]

Nominal, average and exact mass

Three different numbers get called "the mass" and conflating them causes real errors. Nominal mass uses integer nucleon counts. Average mass weights all isotopes by natural abundance and is what a balance measures. Monoisotopic (exact) mass uses the lightest isotope of each element with its actual nuclidic mass, and it is what a high-resolution instrument reports.

The gap between nominal and exact mass is what makes accurate mass useful. CO and N2 are both nominally 28; exactly, they are 27.9949 and 28.0062, a difference of 11.3 mDa, so separating them needs R ≈ 2500. At m/z 400, a 5 ppm measurement is a 2.0 mDa window — narrow enough that only a handful of elemental formulae fit, which is the whole basis of formula assignment from accurate mass.

Careful Resolving power and mass accuracy are different claims and are often confused. Resolving power says whether two peaks are distinguishable; mass accuracy says how close the reported centroid is to the true value. High resolution makes high accuracy achievable but does not deliver it — accuracy also needs calibration, and it degrades when too many ions are in the trap at once, because space charge shifts the very frequencies being measured.[15]

7. Beyond the basics

Hybrids are the norm now. Few serious instruments use one analyser. A Q-TOF puts a quadrupole in front of a TOF to select a precursor and analyse its fragments; an Orbitrap instrument typically has a quadrupole for isolation and a C-trap to accumulate and inject ion packets. The quadrupole survives not for its resolving power but because it is a fast, cheap, robust gate.

Single-stage mirrors are long. The first-order focusing condition derived above, E = 4V/L, forces the ion to penetrate to a depth of L/4 — half the length of one drift leg, which is why the reflectron in the simulation is drawn so deep. Practical instruments use a two-stage mirror: a short, steep retarding stage decelerates the ions most of the way, and a weak second stage does the turning. That shortens the penetration and, with the extra free parameter, allows second-order focusing, which is where resolving powers in the tens of thousands come from.

Space charge is the ceiling on trapping instruments. Coulomb repulsion between trapped ions shifts and broadens frequencies, so the number of ions admitted has to be regulated — which is why automatic gain control exists. This couples resolution, accuracy and dynamic range in a way beam instruments do not experience.[15]

What is still contested. Whether reported resolving powers are comparable between vendors is a live irritation rather than a settled matter: the convention of quoting Orbitrap resolution at m/z 200, where it is highest, is not universally followed for other analysers, and definitions of peak width (FWHM versus the 10% valley criterion) differ by a factor of about two. When comparing instruments, check which definition and which m/z is being quoted before comparing the numbers.

Check yourself

1. A quadrupole is scanning with U/V held at 99.6% of the apex ratio. Roughly what resolving power should you expect, and what would doubling it cost?

Answer
1 − f = 0.004, so R ≈ 0.75/0.004 ≈ 190. Doubling R means halving (1 − f) to 0.002, which halves the width of the transmitted q window — so roughly half the ion current reaches the detector. Resolution in a quadrupole is paid for in sensitivity, almost one for one.

2. You double the length of a linear TOF drift tube. By how much does the resolving power improve?

Answer
Less than twice, and possibly not at all. R = t/2Δt, and doubling L doubles t — but Δt from the initial energy spread also grows with flight distance, because the velocity spread keeps converting into a position spread for longer. This is precisely why reflectrons rather than longer tubes became the answer: the mirror lengthens t while holding Δt almost fixed.[11]

3. A spectrum shows M at 100% and M+2 at 65%, with no significant M+1. What does that tell you?

Answer
Two chlorines. The predicted pattern for Cl2 is 1 : 0.639 : 0.102, and the absence of a meaningful M+1 rules out a large carbon count. Note what this does not tell you: nothing about where the chlorines sit, or even whether both are on the same fragment.

4. An ion is reported at m/z 400.0 with 5 ppm accuracy, and you want its elemental formula. What else do you need?

Answer
The charge state, which the isotope spacing gives you (peaks 1 Da apart mean z = 1, 0.5 Da apart mean z = 2). And then constraints: a 2.0 mDa window at mass 400 still admits many formulae over an unrestricted element set, so in practice you restrict the elements, apply the nitrogen rule and ring-plus-double-bond equivalents, and check the predicted isotope pattern against the observed one. Accurate mass narrows the field; it rarely closes it alone.
