The four-stroke engine and the Otto cycle

A four-stroke engine turns two crankshaft revolutions into one power pulse. The thermodynamics that explains it — the air-standard Otto cycle — delivers one strikingly clean result: ideal efficiency depends on the compression ratio and nothing else. Not the fuel, not the heat released, not the peak temperature. These notes build the mechanism, derive that result, and then account for the roughly 20 efficiency points that separate it from a real engine.

By the end you can
  • relate crank angle to cylinder volume through the slider-crank geometry, and read a four-stroke indicator diagram;
  • analyse each Otto process with the closed-system energy equation and derive η = 1 − r1−γ;
  • compute states, net work, efficiency and mean effective pressure for a given cycle;
  • name the specific mechanisms that take a 60% ideal cycle down to a 40% engine, and size each one.

1. The four strokes

One cycle spans 720° of crank rotation — two revolutions — and four piston travels. Nikolaus Otto built the first successful four-stroke engine in 1876.

StrokeCrank anglePistonValvesWhat happens
Intake0–180°TDC → BDCIntake openDescending piston draws in the fresh charge. Cylinder pressure sits slightly below atmospheric — that deficit is pumping work.
Compression180–360°BDC → TDCBoth shutCharge compressed; pressure and temperature climb. Work goes into the gas.
Power360–540°TDC → BDCBoth shutSpark near TDC; combustion raises pressure sharply, and the gas does work on the piston. The only stroke that pays.
Exhaust540–720°BDC → TDCExhaust openBurned gas pushed out slightly above atmospheric. More pumping work.

Two details the idealisation will quietly discard. Real valves do not open and shut at dead centre: the exhaust valve opens before BDC so blowdown starts early, and the intake valve closes after BDC to exploit charge inertia. Near TDC between exhaust and intake both valves are briefly open together — valve overlap, which scavenges residual gas.

2. Geometry: crank angle to cylinder volume

Everything thermodynamic is a function of volume, and volume is set by the slider-crank. With crank radius a = S/2, connecting-rod length L and crank angle θ measured from TDC, the piston displacement is

x(θ) = a(1 − cosθ) + L(1 − √(1 − λ²sin²θ)),  λ = a/L

and the instantaneous cylinder volume is V(θ) = V_c + A·x(θ) with bore area A = πB²/4. The clearance volume follows from the compression ratio:

r = (V_c + V_d)/V_c  ⇒  V_c = V_d/(r − 1)

Note that V(θ) is not sinusoidal. The √(1 − λ²sin²θ) term makes the piston spend longer near TDC than a pure sinusoid would — which matters, because that is where combustion happens.

4. The air-standard Otto cycle

The idealisation makes the mechanism tractable. The working fluid is air treated as an ideal gas with constant specific heats; the same air is retained throughout, so the gas-exchange strokes vanish; combustion is replaced by external constant-volume heat addition; exhaust is replaced by constant-volume heat rejection. Four processes remain:

ProcessDescriptionEnergy equation (closed system, per unit mass)
1→2Isentropic compressionw12 = u1 − u2 = cv(T1 − T2), q = 0
2→3Constant-volume heat additionqin = u3 − u2 = cv(T3 − T2), w = 0
3→4Isentropic expansionw34 = u3 − u4 = cv(T3 − T4), q = 0
4→1Constant-volume heat rejectionqout = u4 − u1 = cv(T4 − T1), w = 0

Work crosses the boundary only on the two isentropes; heat crosses only on the two isochores. That separation is what makes the algebra collapse.

Deriving the efficiency

Thermal efficiency is net work over heat in, and since the isochores do no work:

η = wnet/qin = 1 − qout/qin = 1 − cv(T4 − T1) / cv(T3 − T2) = 1 − (T4 − T1)/(T3 − T2)

For an isentropic process in an ideal gas with constant specific heats, TVγ−1 = const. Both isentropes span the same volume ratio, V1/V2 = V4/V3 = r, so

T2/T1 = rγ−1  and  T3/T4 = rγ−1  ⇒  T1 = T2r1−γ, T4 = T3r1−γ

Substituting, the factor r1−γ comes out of the numerator and the bracket (T3 − T2) cancels top and bottom:

ηOtto = 1 − r1−γ = 1 − 1/rγ−1

Everything about the combustion has disappeared. The ideal efficiency does not depend on how much heat you add, on the peak temperature, on the peak pressure, or on engine size — only on the compression ratio and the specific heat ratio.

Why knock sets the ceiling.

Compressing the charge raises its temperature as well as its pressure. Push r too high and the end-gas — the mixture ahead of the advancing flame front — reaches its autoignition temperature and detonates spontaneously instead of burning smoothly. The resulting pressure waves are audible and can destroy pistons. This is a chemical kinetics limit on a fuel property (octane rating), not a thermodynamic one, which is why a compression-ignition engine — compressing air alone, with fuel injected only when combustion is wanted — can run at r ≈ 18 and reach higher efficiency.

5. Mean effective pressure, and a worked cycle

Efficiency says nothing about size. Mean effective pressure is the fictitious constant pressure that, acting on the piston through one full power stroke, would produce the same net work as the whole cycle:

MEP = wnet / (v1 − v2) = Wnet / Vd

It is the fair way to compare engines of different displacement: two engines with the same MEP extract the same work per litre swept.

Worked example

An ideal air-standard Otto cycle with r = 9. At state 1, P1 = 100 kPa and T1 = 300 K; heat added is 1800 kJ/kg. Take cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.4.

Compression. rγ−1 = 90.4 = 2.408, so T2 = 300 × 2.408 = 722.5 K and P2 = 100 × 91.4 = 2167 kPa.

Heat addition. T3 = T2 + qin/cv = 722.5 + 1800/0.718 = 3229 K; at constant volume P3 = P2(T3/T2) = 9688 kPa.

Expansion. T4 = T3/2.408 = 1341 K; P4 = P3/91.4 = 447 kPa.

Results. qout = 0.718(1341 − 300) = 747.4 kJ/kg, so wnet = 1800 − 747.4 = 1052.6 kJ/kg and η = 1052.6/1800 = 58.5% — which the closed form 1 − 9−0.4 reproduces exactly, as it must.

For MEP: v1 = RT1/P1 = 0.287(300)/100 = 0.861 m³/kg, v2 = v1/9 = 0.0957, so MEP = 1052.6/0.7653 = 1375 kPa ≈ 13.8 bar.

Read T3 sceptically.

3229 K is above the adiabatic flame temperature of a stoichiometric petrol–air mixture, and well above the point where combustion products dissociate and absorb energy. The constant-specific-heat assumption has broken down — cv for air rises substantially with temperature, so the real T3 is far lower for the same heat input. Textbook Otto problems routinely produce this artefact. Quote it as a model output, never as a cylinder temperature.

6. From 60% to 40%: where it actually goes

A modern production petrol engine reaches around 40% peak brake thermal efficiency, and the average engine about 35% — Toyota's Dynamic Force family claims 40% in the conventional version and 41% in the Atkinson-cycle hybrid version, a world-best for mass production. The ideal cycle at r = 10 promised 60.2%. The gap is not one effect; it is a stack, and they are worth sizing separately.

LossWhy the model misses itRough scale
Variable specific heatsγ falls from 1.4 toward ~1.3 as temperature rises and products dissociate; the model fixes it at the cold-air value.The single largest correction — around 10 points at r = 10
Finite combustion durationHeat release takes 30–60° of crank rotation, not zero. Burning while the piston is already descending gives up expansion work; hence spark advance before TDC as a compromise.Several points
Heat transferCompression and expansion are modelled as adiabatic, but the cylinder is water-cooled and the gas is at ~2500 K. Heat leaves through the walls throughout.Several points
Exhaust blowdownThe valve opens before BDC, so gas at pressure above ambient escapes without completing expansion. The model instead rejects heat at constant volume.A few points
Pumping workThe negative loop in Figure 1. Severe at part load in a throttled petrol engine, where the intake manifold runs well below atmospheric.Small at full load, large at part load

Note what the list implies about engineering strategy. Raising r is attacking the first-order term and is blocked by knock. So the gains of the last two decades came from the others: direct injection to cool the charge and allow higher r, variable valve timing to cut pumping work, Atkinson-style late intake closing to expand more than you compress, cooled EGR to limit peak temperature, and friction reduction everywhere.

Cheat sheet

Geometry

  • x(θ) = a(1 − cosθ) + L(1 − √(1 − λ²sin²θ)),  a = S/2,  λ = a/L
  • V(θ) = Vc + A·x(θ);  A = πB²/4;  Vd = A·S
  • r = (Vc + Vd)/Vc  ⇒  Vc = Vd/(r − 1)
  • One cycle = 720° crank = 2 revolutions = 1 power stroke

Cycle

  • 1→2 isentropic compression · 2→3 constant-volume heat addition · 3→4 isentropic expansion · 4→1 constant-volume heat rejection
  • Isentropic: TVγ−1 = const,  PVγ = const,  T2/T1 = rγ−1,  P2/P1 = rγ
  • qin = cv(T3 − T2),  qout = cv(T4 − T1),  wnet = qin − qout
  • η = 1 − r1−γ — depends on r and γ only
  • MEP = wnet/(v1 − v2) = Wnet/Vd

Numbers

  • Air: cv = 0.718, cp = 1.005, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.4 (cold air-standard)
  • ηideal: r = 8 → 56.5% · r = 10 → 60.2% · r = 12 → 63.0%
  • Petrol r typically 8:1 to 12:1 (knock-limited); diesel r ≈ 18
  • Real petrol engine: ~35% average, ~40–41% best in production; Toyota GD diesel claims 44%
  • Combustion products: γ → ~1.3, costing ~10 points at r = 10

Traps

  • η is independent of qin — adding more heat raises work and MEP, not efficiency
  • Otto η is not Carnot η; compare only at matched temperature limits
  • Textbook T3 values above ~2800 K are artefacts of constant cv
  • Indicated ≠ brake: friction has not been counted until you say "brake"

Self-test

Two engines have the same compression ratio; one runs rich and releases far more heat per cycle. Which has the higher ideal efficiency?

Neither — they are equal. η = 1 − r1−γ contains no heat term. The engine releasing more heat produces more net work per cycle and therefore a higher MEP and more power, but it also rejects proportionally more heat, and the ratio is unchanged. Confusing "more work" with "more efficient" is the single most common error with this cycle.

Why does the pumping loop run anticlockwise, and what does that signify?

During intake the pressure is below atmospheric while the volume increases; during exhaust it is above atmospheric while the volume decreases. Traversed in that order the loop encloses area in the negative sense, so ∮P dV is negative: the engine does net work on the gas to breathe. It is subtracted from the gross indicated work. Throttling a petrol engine at part load deepens the intake depression and enlarges this loop, which is why part-load efficiency is poor and why variable valve timing and lean/unthrottled strategies pay off.

Spark fires before TDC, during compression. Doesn't that oppose the piston and waste work?

Partly, yes — and that is the compromise. Combustion takes 30–60° of crank angle, so igniting exactly at TDC would release most of the heat while the piston was already well down the bore, at a large volume where the pressure rise does far less work. Advancing the spark puts peak pressure shortly after TDC, where the crank has good leverage. Too much advance and the negative compression work plus knock risk outweigh the gain; the optimum is MBT — minimum advance for best torque.

You double the displacement at fixed r, fixed MEP and fixed speed. What happens to efficiency and to power?

Efficiency is unchanged: it depends on r and γ, and MEP is work per swept volume, so work per cycle scales with displacement while heat input scales with it too. Power roughly doubles, since Wnet = MEP × Vd per cycle at the same cycle rate. This is exactly why MEP, not work, is the right currency for comparing engines.
