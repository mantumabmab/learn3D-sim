Why is the sky blue?

Sunlight is secretly every colour at once. The air picks out the blue and throws it all over the sky — and that is the whole trick.

By the end of these notes you can…

  • tell someone what sunlight is really made of;
  • explain why blue gets spread across the sky and red mostly does not;
  • work out for yourself why sunsets go orange, and why there is no blue sky on the Moon.

1. Sunlight is a rainbow in disguise

Sunlight looks white or pale yellow. It is not. It is every colour of the rainbow travelling together, all mixed up. When the colours are mixed, your eyes just see "white".

You can catch them coming apart. A rainbow does it with raindrops. A glass of water on a sunny windowsill does it too, and so does the edge of a CD.

Try this

  1. Find a rainbow, a prism, a garden sprinkler, or a shiny CD in sunlight.
  2. Before you look: which colour do you think will be at the top of the rainbow, and which at the bottom? Say your guess out loud.
  3. Now look. Red is on the outside, violet on the inside. Every colour was hiding inside the plain white light the whole time.

2. Light travels as waves — some choppy, some long and lazy

Light moves like a wave, a bit like a wiggle sent down a skipping rope. Here is the important part: each colour wiggles a different amount.

Blue light is a short, choppy wave. Red light is a long, lazy wave. Scientists call the length of one wiggle the wavelength.

Both waves are drawn crossing the same distance. The blue one fits far more wiggles in.

3. The air is full of specks too small to see

The air around you is not empty. It is packed with tiny pieces of gas called molecules — mostly nitrogen and oxygen. They are unbelievably small: more than a thousand times smaller than one wiggle of light. You will never see one.

When a wave of light bumps into one of these specks, the speck can fling the light off sideways, in a brand new direction. That flinging-off is called scattering.

And here is the heart of the whole thing: short choppy waves get flung around much more easily than long lazy ones.

How much more? Do the sums for blue light against red light, and blue gets scattered nearly 6 times as much. Violet, choppier still, gets flung about 9 times as much as red.

So when sunlight pours into the air, the red, orange and yellow mostly sail straight on through. The blue gets bounced from speck to speck to speck, until it is coming at you from every part of the sky at once.

You see blue above you because blue is the colour that got lost in the air.

5. So why do sunsets go orange and red?

Nothing changes about the Sun in the evening. What changes is how much air the light has to push through to get to you.

When the Sun is high above your head, its light takes a short cut straight down — one helping of air. When the Sun sits on the horizon, the light comes in at a long slant, skimming sideways through about 37 times as much air.

On a journey that long, nearly all the blue gets scattered away before it reaches you. It is bounced off sideways and ends up lighting the sky for somebody else, far away. What is left to arrive at your eyes is the long, lazy, hard-to-scatter light: orange and red.

A sunset is simply what sunlight looks like once the air has stolen the blue out of it.

6. Why the sky is palest down near the ground

Look straight up on a clear day: deep, strong blue. Now look out at the horizon: pale, washed-out, almost white.

Straight up, you are looking through the thinnest slice of air there is. Towards the horizon you are looking the long way, through heaps of air. The blue light in there gets scattered again, and again, and again — and light bounced that many times comes at you from all directions and in all colours mixed together. All the colours mixed together is exactly what white means.

7. Hang on — violet is even choppier. Why isn't the sky purple?

Good catch. This is the question that catches out most grown-ups.

Violet light is scattered even more than blue. So the sky really does have plenty of violet in it. Two things stop you seeing purple:

  • the Sun sends out less violet light to begin with than blue, so there is simply less of it to spread about;
  • the colour detectors inside your eyes are not very good at violet. They are much more excited by blue.

So the sky is a mixture — lots of blue, some violet, a little of everything else — and your eyes report the whole mixture to your brain as "blue". The sky's colour is partly about the air, and partly about your eyes. Another animal, with different eyes, would not see quite the colour you see.

8. No air, no blue sky

If the blue comes from air, then somewhere with no air should have no blue sky at all. And that is exactly what we find.

Astronauts standing on the Moon saw a black sky, with stars in it, in the middle of the day with the Sun blazing above them. The Moon has almost no air, so there is nothing there to scatter sunlight sideways into your eyes. The sunlight just goes straight past you and carries on.

Mars does it back to front

Mars has a thin air, full of fine dust. Dust is far bigger than a gas molecule and it scatters light in its own way. NASA's landers have photographed the result: the Martian sky is orange-ish in the daytime, and turns blue-grey around the Sun at sunset — the opposite way round from Earth.

A planet's sky colour is a clue about what its air is made of. That is a genuinely useful thing for scientists, not just a pretty one.

Show what you know

Have a go at answering before you open each one.

1. What colour is sunlight really?

All of them at once. Sunlight carries every colour of the rainbow mixed together, which your eyes read as white.

2. Why does blue end up all over the sky instead of red?

Blue travels as short, choppy waves, and short waves are flung sideways by the tiny specks of gas in the air far more easily — nearly 6 times as much as red. The red light mostly carries straight on.

3. Your friend says the sky is blue because it reflects the sea. What do you say?

That the sky is just as blue over the middle of a desert, thousands of kilometres from any sea — so it cannot be coming from the water. The blue is made in the air itself.

4. Why is the Moon's daytime sky black?

There is almost no air on the Moon, so there are no specks to scatter sunlight sideways into an astronaut's eyes. No scattering, no sky colour — just black, with stars.

5. Harder: if Earth's air were twice as thick, what would a sunset look like?

Reasonable answer: even redder, and darker. More air means more scattering, so blue and green would be stripped out even sooner, leaving deeper red — and less light getting through overall. (Dust and smoke in the air do something similar, which is why sunsets after a big wildfire can be an astonishing red.)

Big words, small meanings

Molecule
A tiny piece of a substance. Air is made of them; they are thousands of times smaller than a wave of light.
Scatter
To fling light off in a new direction instead of letting it go straight on.
Wavelength
The length of one wiggle of a light wave. Blue has short wiggles, red has long ones.
Atmosphere
The blanket of air wrapped around a planet.
Rayleigh scattering
The grown-up name for what this whole page is about: light being scattered by specks much smaller than its own wiggle. Named after Lord Rayleigh, who worked out the sums in the 1870s.
