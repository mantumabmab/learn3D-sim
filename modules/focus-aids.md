# Module: focus aids

**Use this module only when it was asked for:** the person mentions ADHD, attention, focus, "I lose track", "I get
distracted", "short chunks", or picks focus aids at the confirm step. Otherwise write standard notes
(`references/register.md`) and skip this file. Levels 1-2 already get a child layout; this module adds to it.

## What switching it on changes

1. Front matter `focus: yes`. The study page then gets Focus mode (one section at a time), a progress bar with
   Mark-as-done, the 12+3 / 24+6 study timer, Read-aloud and the "Start in focus mode" button; the checker enforces
   short sections (about 220-500 words by level, ≤ 6 minutes with figure and questions) and a question after every one.
2. Writing, on top of the level's register (the register stays: a graduate still gets graduate terminology and
   numbers, just in smaller pieces):
   - every section opens with `:::bigidea`; one idea per section, headed by its claim
   - `:::simple` / `:::expert` for the same explanation at two depths
   - 1-3 `:::check` questions after every section; a mixed review at the end
   - `{{term|definition}}` tap-to-define for every term, so nobody has to scroll to a glossary
   - worked examples revealed one step at a time
3. Never name the reason in the notes ("for ADHD people"): the design just works.

## The evidence behind it

These rules are for learners whose attention slips: children, and adults with ADHD or ADHD-like focus problems. Working memory is the bottleneck. Meta-analyses find large working-memory deficits
in children with ADHD and moderate ones that persist in adults [Kasper 2012; Alderson 2013]. So every rule below
does one of three things: **cut load on working memory**, **make the learner retrieve and act**, or **make progress
and the finish line visible**. Retrieval practice helps students with ADHD as much as anyone [Minear 2023], so the
same well-evidenced techniques apply, with less tolerance for clutter.

## Rules, and why

1. **Big idea first.** Every section opens with its point in 1-2 sentences. An advance summary tells the reader what
   to hold on to (Mayer's signalling and pre-training principles) [Mayer 2020].
2. **Small chunks the learner controls.** One idea per section, under ~5 minutes; Next/Back at the learner's pace
   (segmenting principle) [Mayer 2020]. Focus mode shows one section at a time.
3. **Words + pictures, placed together.** Explanations sit right next to the figure they describe, and labels sit on
   the parts, not in a legend (multimedia and spatial-contiguity principles) [Mayer 2020].
4. **Nothing decorative.** Interesting-but-irrelevant facts, jokes and ornaments reliably reduce learning (the
   seductive-details effect) [Rey 2012]. A fun fact stays only if it carries the concept.
5. **Retrieve, don't reread.** 1-3 recall questions after every chunk, answers hidden until the learner commits,
   immediate explanatory feedback. Practice testing is one of the two highest-utility study techniques [Dunlosky 2013;
   Roediger 2006]. Free recall before multiple choice where possible.
6. **Mix and space.** The wrap-up mixes questions from all sections (interleaving) [Rohrer 2007] and tells the learner
   to come back tomorrow and in a week (distributed practice) [Cepeda 2006; Dunlosky 2013].
7. **Worked examples step by step.** For calculations, show the solution one step at a time before asking for one
   [Sweller 1985]; the page reveals steps on demand so nothing runs ahead of the learner.
8. **Give the figure a job.** Every 3D figure comes with "Try this" prompts that ask for a prediction before the press
   ("What will happen to cAMP when you hydrolyse GTP?"). Active, guided manipulation, not free play.
9. **Short, structured breaks, opt-in.** The timer offers 12+3 or 24+6 minute blocks. In a field study, students on
   fixed breaks reported less fatigue and distraction and finished the same work in less time than students who
   chose their own breaks [Biwer 2023]. Never a countdown that pressures.
10. **Read-aloud and spacing.** Text-to-speech gave a small-to-moderate comprehension benefit for students with reading
    difficulties [Wood 2018]; wider letter spacing helped children with dyslexia read faster and more accurately
    [Zorzi 2012]. Both are switches, so nobody gets them unless they want them.
11. **Visible progress and a finish line.** Goals on the cover, minutes per section, a progress bar, Mark-as-done.

## Popular, but not supported: leave out

- **"Learning styles" (visual/auditory/kinaesthetic learners).** No good evidence that matching teaching to a style
  helps [Pashler 2008]. Give everyone words *and* pictures.
- **Bionic Reading (bolding the first half of each word).** A controlled test found no reading-speed benefit
  [Snell 2024].
- Walls of text, auto-playing animation, flashing elements, gamified points that reward speed over thinking.

## Respectful framing

Never label the reader ("for ADHD people") in the notes themselves; the design just works for them. Don't promise
the notes treat or fix anything. If a person mentions their own ADHD, take it as context for pacing and format,
nothing more.
